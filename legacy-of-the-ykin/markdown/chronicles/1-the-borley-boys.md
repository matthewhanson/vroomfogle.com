# Chapter 1: The Borley Boys

Our chronicles begin in **Ixtal**—the **Yinka**’s hidden region of cliffs and scrubby hills in southern **Tanara**—where distance is a weapon and secrecy is policy. Here, the **Priest-Lords** don’t merely rule: they manufacture. They turn boys into knives, girls into rifle-scopes, and scholars into conduits.

**Borley Monastery** and the **Temple of Chakor** stand as the cleanest symbols of that work: *Borley* breeding assassins and infiltrators, *Chakor* shaping minds and nerves with the cold precision of **Essænce**. Beyond them are the other institutes—**Nakbe** with its demon-contact and artifact obsession; **Tikal**’s maternal cloisters and their snipers and gadgets; **Ixkun**’s Abbey where devotion becomes tangible force.

All of it is stitched together by the same hand. The **Priest-Lords** dictate curricula, assignments, loyalties. They keep the world beyond **Ixtal** at arm’s length, and keep their own people in tighter.

And then, when the training is done, they send their graduates out into the world as if letting an arrow loose.

## Graduation

Borley didn’t throw celebrations. It threw bodies.

Four graduates survived long enough to earn names spoken aloud: **Kawill**, **Bloth’roc**, **Zalcoat**, and **Belthar**. Their final examination was not a written test or a lecture. It was a duel—peer against peer, blade against breath—until only the living remained to be used.

Their first mission came immediately, delivered without warmth: travel to the Temple of Chakor and present themselves to the Priest-Lords for further instruction.

It was, they were told, critical to the completion of The Plan. They stocked equipment, took rations, and captured offerings—at least two live cliff gark, ape-like humanoids from the high places. The captures did not come clean. The garks fought like bad weather, and the road to Chakor left the group scraped and bleeding in ways Borley would have called “minor.”

*Chakor* did not call anything minor. Inside the temple there was no welcome, no congratulations—only straps and cots, as if they were specimens to be catalogued.

The priests tested them for weakness to the **Essænce**. Some attacks were invasive—voices that weren’t theirs, thoughts with foreign edges. Others were brutally physical: sudden bleeding, cracked bone, pain that seemed to bloom everywhere at once.

And then came the accusation.

**Kulkan**—older, senior to them, late-*Borley* and now newly priest—named **Zalcoat** a heretic. He claimed **Zalcoat** had been working with **Trell**, the Cook of *Borley*. He claimed, too, that he knew **Zalcoat**’s thoughts.

The bonds of shared training didn’t matter. There was nowhere to step in.

**Ba’bak**, high-ranking and unsoftened, ended discussion by speaking a word that sounded wrong in the mouth. A black void rimmed in red gathered around his hands and **Zalcoat**’s body—an effect the others were already learning to fear.

Zalcoat screamed.

Then Ba’bak drew brass knuckles from his robes and beat Zalcoat’s face until it was no longer a face.  Motion stopped and the floor was wet. Zalcoat was taken away.

The survivors were unstrapped, bathed, and given clean beds—formalities that felt almost mocking. They had arrived at Chakor expecting the usual, Borley-shaped absence of celebration. What they got instead was triage by cruelty: strapped down, tested, and injured deliberately.

Some of it was mental—voices threading into their thoughts, foreign impressions trying to take root. Some of it was viscera and bone—spontaneous bleeding, cracked ribs, pain across the body like a net tightening.

Ba’bak called it “to determine your strengths and weakness to the Essænce,” and shared none of what he learned. Then the accusation: Kulkan, the former Borley adherent and now a fledgling priest, declared Zalcoat a heretic—claimed he conspired with Trell, the Cook of Borley, and claimed to know Zalcoat’s thoughts. Kulkan had been inside their heads; the implication landed like a threat: there were no secrets left.

Ba’bak spoke a disturbing word. A black void rimmed in red gathered around his hands and Zalcoat’s body. Pain wracked Zalcoat as he screamed. Then Ba’bak produced brass knuckles and beat Zalcoat’s face until it stopped being a face.

Zalcoat was taken away. The survivors were released, given baths, and offered sleep—if they could close their eyes at all.

## The Temple of Chakor

After the trials, the survivors were brought together again—three companions now, their fourth reduced to a cautionary story. They were granted an audience with Ba’bak, and with him the part of The Plan they were permitted to know.

The **Yinka** army would move north to raid the southern **Myri** villages. The **Myri**’s agricultural wealth would fund conquest, and the loss of that labor would, in time, starve and weaken the **Duranaki**—an underground civilization dependent on **Myri** hands and **Myri** harvest.

The **Sulini**—the coastal fishers—were not targets. Trade made them useful; useful things were tolerated.

To slip into **Myri** villages and survive long enough to do damage, **Ba’bak** provided a tool that made skin into disguise: **Adzel’s Skull**, a relic that could take a **Myri**’s face and produce a mask. Wear it and you wore the dead—their appearance, their language.

The Plan itself was not new; every Y’kin knew of it if not its details. Yugal, Father of Gods, spoke to the people through High Priest Mateshe, who ruled through the Inner Conclave. The Y’kin were the chosen people. All others were unsalvageable beings—tolerated right up until they interfered.

On a cold morning after the Trials, they ate fruit, fish, and bread in a small courtyard with Ba’bak and the prior, Xoc. Ba’bak spoke of the next stage: years of preparation, armies built, and special operatives trained in places like Borley.

There had been a thousand years of peace between the Y’kin and the Myri. That ended this spring. The Myri—hardworking, agricultural, rich in harvest—would be dominated and used to feed the war effort. Perhaps more importantly, cutting Myri freedom would eventually starve the Duranaki of labor and weaken part of their defensive force.

The Sulini would be tolerated for now because trade made them valuable: goods and fish, routes and rumors.

Ba’bak’s orders were simple. They would act as infiltrators and scouts as armies moved north. Kulkan was their leader. If anything happened to Kulkan—or if only two of them remained—they were to find the nearest Navigator Obelisk and return to Katchere as fast as possible.

He issued a tube holding a map of obelisk locations and a bundle of Sel-kai paper money—useful, perhaps, in cities, and likely worthless in the rural places they were headed.

**Armand** joined them as guide and tracker—useful hands, sharp eyes, and the kind of practical competence that made him valuable to priests who rarely valued people. He carried a short sword and a bow, partial to the bow the way cautious men often were. Winter ebbed. The road called.

Before they left, they were seated for a dinner made significant by who attended it: Denivor, a Sulini trader, accompanied by two Changramai monks—one Yinka, the other Jameri from western Jaiman. Conversation circled like a knife above a table, revealing rumors gathered over the last decade in Tanara.

Denivor’s decade of tales came out in layers. Ten years ago, a winter of ice and snow refused to end. Spring stayed frozen. Summer stayed cold. Harvests failed, and famine spread across Jaiman.

In Tanara, the Sulini managed by fishing and trade—enough, even, to support many Myri through good will. The Yinka suffered more. The Duranaki kept their underground gardens alive, but faced incursions of Snow Garks swarming out of the mountains.

Denivor spoke of politics, too. The Sulini and Myri were easy to read. The Duranaki were not: wealthy families, an elected First Speaker, and a shift in power from K’ya Agarian to T’kaal Arain, hero of the Ice-Winter. K’ya vanished after losing. Some believed T’kaal wanted expansion.

Most interesting were the rumors Denivor treated as carefully true: sightings of the ancient Cloudlords—or people like them—riding majestic Steardan. Ba’bak dismissed it publicly, but later conversations suggested he knew the truth was complicated. Denivor believed the Cloudlords were not only seen, but in talks with the Duranaki.

Beyond Tanara he spoke of Rhakhaan: Queen Italana’s death in 6043; Jerrin III refusing the Phoenix Crown; Frelik stealing it and retreating north to claim legitimacy; Wiliris revolting; and Frelik the Pretender crawling south with mercenary help.

And he spoke of U-Lyshak’s unraveling—Helyssa’s Prince Halek killed in a “hunting accident,” his son Kier disappearing, and a Priest of Yarth stepping in as regent to keep anarchy from swallowing what remained.

They set out with **Denivor** and his bodyguards, heading toward **Tartok**. On the road, **Zuma**—the **Yinka** **Changramai**—sparred with **Kawill** in the manner of men who call violence “practice.” **Kawill** fared poorly.

Then Tartok came into view, and “practice” lost its meaning. The village was dead.

Not the clean dead of an army’s raid, but the strange dead of a lone rider on horseback—if the evidence could be believed. Charred prints marked the earth where hooves had struck, as if the horse had carried fire in its bones. The massacre looked to have happened two nights prior.

There were tracks leading away—maybe survivors, maybe looters, maybe something worse. No one suggested staying.

In truth, Ba'bak knew the Cloudlords were more than rumors, according to Armand, and said so to Denivor in the
language of the Sulini. Denivor later confirmed this when you traveled together to the village of Tartok. The
Cloudlords have not only been seen, Denivor believes they have entered talks with the Duranaki. Ba'bak clearly did not
want you to know this, and Armand should probably be treated with contempt for telling you this.

## Intrigue at Ixtal

Trulka was small—only a few hundred souls—but it carried the weight of being a place people passed through on their way to the war. It had a library that mattered, a tavern called the Fat Louse, and the particular strangeness of a town whose able-bodied men had been taken north while the remaining folk kept the world going anyway.

They arrived through the remnants of a recent skirmish and were set loose for a day at Ba’bak’s instruction: Kulkan to speak with priests, others to tend to needs that Borley trained out of them but never fully removed.

Kulkan’s interest in the library wasn’t academic. Ba’bak believed it housed a key tied to Krelij—a legendary smith who forged weapons said to turn wars. The texts suggested an underground forge north of Tanara might be a resting place for those artifacts.

The library’s scribes were all women, tradition held like law in that place. They were autonomous within their walls until a priest spoke and reminded them they were not.

Kaleel, head scribe, timid and demure until her pride sharpened, swore she’d seen references to the key. She also swore it wasn’t there.

Ba’bak’s instruction for Trulka was blunt: spend the day digging.

With Kulkan’s authority as Coadjutor of Ixtal, there were no doors they weren’t allowed to open.

They reviewed the original writings Kaleel had provided. They questioned scribes and townsfolk about suspicious travelers. They investigated the tunnels beneath the library.

Kaleel repeated the same central fact: Ba’bak had only ever heard rumors of a key tied to one of Krelij’s secondary forges. A text described it, but the key itself was not in the archives.

The scribes did copy manuscripts referencing the Narsaelkin and Krelij. A rough map of Tanara came with notes pointing north: an old underground forge in the foothills, north of Myri lands and in the Duranaki heartland, believed to be used by Krelij and possibly a resting place for the Narsaelkin. Krelij’s main forge was historically placed in Wuliris, but it wasn’t believed the Narsaelkin were there.

A hidden entrance in the lower levels of the library led them down—not into a secret room, but into a long spiral staircase and a network of tunnels that ran deep enough to be under the lake.

No tool marks. No chisel scars. The rock looked as if it had been removed rather than cut.

Three main branches presented themselves.

One ran only ten or twenty minutes before rising again: a spiral staircase ending in a small space hidden in the walls of a house in town. The home belonged to Trulka’s main blacksmith, Ahtok—gone with the army—and was now inhabited by his daughter, Dirtka. Dirtka seemed absent of anything resembling a thought; she was only scared and confused as strangers appeared, impossibly, in her bedroom.

Another tunnel went on for hours before ending at an impassable drop: a massive void with a bottom beyond their lantern light. At some point the artificial passage connected with a natural tunnel that felt like a dry underground riverbed. Far ahead, water thundered—an underground waterfall spilling into a large subterranean cavern.

The final tunnel they investigated in the evening, without Armand. After hours of walking—and after the first creeping thought that they should turn back—they found a cave that opened to the night sky. In one corner sat a pile of saddlebags packed with clothing and armor appropriate for priests, scribes, and Y’kin army soldiers.

Stepping out into muck, they found themselves at the edge of a body of water. Kulkan judged it to be north of Trulka, along the shore of the same lake. A small cliff rose beside them. From the outside, the cave was invisible; from the right angle, the cliff simply let them walk through.

The next day they woke and waited for priests to arrive so they could continue on.

And the rains began.

Meanwhile, Armand was approached by Hadwin and Akna during a special “encounter” with Dirtka. For reasons unknown, they entrusted him with an intricately crafted key, emphasizing its immense importance and the necessity for utmost secrecy. They specifically warned him against revealing its existence to Kulkan. Armand, unfamiliar with both the key's significance and the intentions of the trio, found himself thrust into a mysterious and potentially perilous situation, the depth of which he was yet to grasp.

The war’s machinery showed itself, too.

Some say wars are won or lost by logistics more than blades, and in Ixtal that belief had a name: Hadwin, the Y’kin army quartermaster, born of Tartok—yes, the same Tartok that was now a dead town.

Hadwin had passed through only a day prior with wagons full of blacksmiths, tanners, craftsmen, and supplies—likely the last of the stragglers who’d tried to avoid being consumed by war and were gathered anyway. They stayed a day, took additional material from local craftsmen, then continued on through Achren and north to join the main army.

After their discoveries and exploration, the group, now back in Trulka, anticipates the arrival of additional priests to guide their next steps. As they wait, the rains begin, potentially foreshadowing challenges ahead.

### People of Interest in Trulka

* **Ch'kan**: The Prior of Trulka who is arrogant and dismissive of anything concerning Ba'bak, including Kulkan and his companions. He disregards scrolls and a key, believing them to detract from a bigger plan.  
* **Kaleel** \- Head Scribe at Ixtal. Timid and demur, a certain arrogance lurks just beneath the surface and comes out at times as condescending. Kaleel got her post because she is a master scholar with a near perfect memory.  
* **Livinous Adal**: A half-breed with an unusual name, Livinous is Ch'kan's overburdened assistant, dressed in gray, tattered priestly robes. He's responsible for the town's administration, from tax collection to settling disputes. He also assigns companions to the town's women.  
* **Bartleby**: A young and healthy man, runs the Fat Louse inn, which has a literary theme. He's curiously not involved in the war and only gives vague reasons when questioned, such as “I’d prefer not to”.  
* **Toktam**: A young boy who is well-connected in the town. He works in various roles, from farming to assisting at the Fat Louse, and even provides entertainment with his lute.

The library’s culture had its own quiet rigidity.

All the scribes were women—not because of the war, but because tradition made it doctrine in practice. The Y’kin had a deep academic history paired with an equally deep history of censorship, and while famous scholars were remembered as men, women had long been the stewards of knowledge in the libraries.

They cared for themselves and their charges; they cooked, cleaned, maintained their own walls. Inside the library they were autonomous in a way most people in Ixtal were not.

They still answered to priests.

At Ixtal—the largest of Y’kin libraries—there were twelve scribes. They spoke little, showed respect, and had little contact with the general populace. None would acknowledge knowledge of the key or the copied documents.

Talking with townsfolk filled in the shape of Trulka’s days. Most war-related traffic had passed through weeks ago. Now the travelers were farmers and agricultural merchants preparing for spring. Increased demands had been placed on the peasantry: quotas, resources, the burden of war without the able-bodied men who’d been taken north.

Trade still existed, but it was the grim trade of necessity: seeds, tools, and beasts of burden—rare now, and expensive.

## Ambush!

They were leaving Trulka when Armand and Dirtka caught up with them.

Armand’s eyes were wrong.

He’d met an old man—one of those strangers who carries curses like coins—and now his vision had been inverted. He could see clearly at night, as if the world had agreed to be readable. In daylight he was nearly blind.

Then he produced the key.

Hadwin and Akna had pressed it into his hands in a strange encounter involving Dirtka and insisted it be kept secret, kept safe—kept from Ba’bak, specifically. The warning had been sharp enough to feel like the edge of a larger blade.

Dirtka was shaken, speaking of her father and a hidden passage in their house that had become dangerous. There was fear in her that wasn’t performative.

Ahead, a broken supply wagon blocked part of the road—an ambush dressed as misfortune. The first bolt found Kulkan.

It hit with the ugly finality of crossbow work, driving deep and leaving him badly wounded. The group struck back—some cleanly, some clumsily, but with the inevitability Borley had branded into them.

And in the chaos, Dirtka moved in a way that made her impossible to ignore.

When it was over, they regrouped around the damage. Kulkan needed urgent attention. Armand, half-blind, worked by feel and memory and steady hands to keep him alive.

Dirtka fled, then reappeared, then fled again. They pursued, and the confrontation that followed ended with truth in motion: Dirtka slipping into a shadowy form, less like a person and more like a hole where a person had been.

That night, Kulkan held Adzel's Skull with a reverence befitting such a powerful artifact. There was a palpable tension in the air as his companions watched, a mixture of unease and curiosity evident in their expressions. Kulkan began chanting in an archaic tongue, his voice weaving an incantation that resonated with the arcane etchings of the skull.

Using the blood of the deceased assassin, he traced elaborate patterns onto the skull, which began to glow dimly, emitting a soft, humming sound. Slowly, from the top of the skull, a flesh-like substance began to emerge, gradually taking the form of a face. It was a gruesome sight, the face molding and reshaping, eyes hollow and mouth slack. Within moments, the transformation was complete, and Kulkan carefully peeled the face from the skull. The face-mask was eerily lifelike, capturing the very essence of the deceased, the lines of experience and battle scars etched into its skin.

Taking a deep breath, Kulkan then placed the mask upon his own face. As it adhered to his skin, his features began to warp and shift, taking on the appearance of the fallen assassin. His voice, when he spoke, was no longer his own but that of Commander Aapo. A flood of fragmented memories, thoughts, and emotions rushed into his mind – glimpses of the commander's life, his allegiances, and possibly, his plans. The transformation is temporary, lasting only 10 days, but the intelligence and strategic advantage it offers are invaluable. It can be used to create more faces for others, if they are willing to undergo the gruesome ritual.

## The Marketplace

They took the road toward Achren by way of Yuka, rain stalking them like a patient hunter.

In ancient ruins half-swallowed by time, they found a market that shouldn’t have existed—canvas and smoke and barter set amid stone that remembered older empires.

There they met Akto—one of the Tun triplets. He recognized Armand. He also had a mission of his own: acquire Shaskan poison for the war.

The venom was deadly. Precisely because of that, it was valuable.

They agreed to help in exchange for a portion.

They tracked the Shaskan into a rain-drenched glade and got ambushed for their trouble. The fight was close and brutal, full of slippery footing and sudden lunges.

Kawill took the worst of it—severe injuries, poison in the blood.

Nawiss appeared during the chaos like a story walking in from another chapter. He was old, accented, strange. He acted as if he belonged there.

In the end, Armand, Belthar, and Akto landed decisive blows and brought several Shaskan down. But victory didn’t clean the wounds.

Belthar showed the early signs of poisoning. Kawill was failing fast.

Akto moved with urgency, taking Kawill to seek an antidote derived from the Blenx berry. The group split: mission and mercy pulling in different directions.

And Nawiss—without debate, without permission—simply began traveling with them.

In hindsight it was not the best idea to hunt the Shaskan.

One wrong move, one lucky strike, and disaster found them. A particularly vicious Shaskan hit Kawill twice, and Kawill went down gravely ill.

Immediately after the battle, the old man—Nawiss—moved to Kawill, mumbling. The sound thickened and reverberated as he approached, and then a silver-white light surrounded Kawill, healing his wounds.

Nawiss, thick-accented, said there was nothing he could do for the poison. It would consume Kawill in hours.

Akto had Neta herb. It wasn’t a cure, but it slowed even potent poisons, and it stabilized Kawill.

Without the true antidote—derived from the Blenx berry—Kawill would die. Even with it, reversal might not be complete; nerve damage could remain.

Akto knew where to find the berry and said he had already notified one of his brothers to help. He worked quickly over the Shaskan corpses, draining poison into glass vials and collecting nine eggs.

Then mission asserted itself. After discussion, Kulkan decided they could not afford more time off-mission—too risky, Kulkan still recovering, and limited time left wearing this face.

They would proceed to Achren. Akto—and presumably his brother—would attempt to revive Kawill and follow. If everything went well, they would arrive before the party left Achren for the front.

## The Hounds and the Storm

Yuka received them in a downpour.

They had two options for reaching Oxak: a wagon road that would take a full day, or the river rapids—faster, uglier, and more likely to kill them.

They took the river.

Most tried to find shelter in an inn called the Brown Dragon, only to learn it now bore a new name—The Boot Swap—earned by whatever happened to the last owner. Nawiss and Bloth’roc slept in a barn. In the night, Nawiss performed a quiet ritual over nearby crops as if the land itself had to be addressed properly.

Morning brought no mercy from the sky. They hired a guide named Ezco and paid for a boat.

The journey should have taken less than four hours.

Instead, an Essænce storm rolled over the river without warning.

Whirlpools formed like mouths.

And Zephyr hounds appeared—wolf-like shapes with luminous eyes, water-breathing predators moving through the chaos like they’d been born in it.

Most of the group managed to get off the boat. Nawiss, Bloth’roc, and Belthaar stayed aboard long enough for the fight to become desperate.

Spells snapped into the rain.

Arrows vanished into spray. Steel struck wet fur.

Armand took a bolt of water that caved armor and knocked him unconscious.

They killed the hounds.

The storm ended as abruptly as it began, leaving the river normal in a way that felt insulting.

Afterward, Nawiss produced herbs from his stores and helped bind them back together.

They continued downriver through forested banks and gray light. By the sixty-seventh day they were set to arrive at Oxak—a town on the river with a road leading to Achren.

Achren waited beyond that road, perched on stone and rumor: a city on a cliff with a waterfall cutting its center, second-largest in the Yinka Empire and far too important to be kind.

## The town of Oxak

They reached Oxak to find damage still raw from a recent Essænce storm. Walls buckled. Docks splintered. Soldiers and priests shouted over each other, trying to make order out of wreckage.

They presented themselves as agents of the Ixtal Institutes under Commander Aapo—Kulkan wearing the borrowed face—and in that disguise they were tolerated. Nawiss was dismissed as “just an old man.”

In the noise at the docks they caught another thread: the Locusts, a faction fractured by internal disagreement. Some spoke of annihilating the Priesthood. Some spoke of alliances with other cultures in Tanara. Either way, dissent had a name.

They were ordered to stay at a specific inn—The Bloody Gull—until further instructions arrived.

Dirtka found them there.

She stared at Commander Aapo as if looking at a ghost, because in her hometown he was supposed to be a wanted man. She warned “Aapo” to consult with Quartermaster Hadwin and then proceed to Achren with a key, where an ally would meet them.

In a private chamber at the Bloody Gull, Hadwin arrived—discreet, efficient—his sister Akna at his shoulder.

Hadwin spoke as if he’d been waiting for this version of events: he expressed delight that Aapo survived and that Kulkan was apparently out of the picture. It became clear, in that same casual tone, that Hadwin was tied to the Locusts.

He gave instructions without explanation. Go to Achren. Find Amaraq, marked by a wolf tattoo on his neck. Amaraq would be with someone recognizable but not yet met—one of the Tun brothers.

Kulkan and Armand left the meeting carrying more uncertainty than answers. Leaving Oxak should have been simple.

It wasn’t.

Guards had been told to detain them for further questioning. The gate was a funnel of eyes.

Armand, amateur animal whisperer on his best day, chose panic as strategy.

He spooked the donkey.

The animal bucked and brayed, turning the square into a small riot. Stalls overturned. Goods scattered. Guards surged toward the chaos.

The party slipped through the gate under the cover of the commotion.

From then on, the donkey stayed close to Armand as if convinced he was kin.
