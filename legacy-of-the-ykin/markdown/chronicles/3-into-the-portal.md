# Chapter 3: Into the Portal

The melody did not echo so much as it *caught*—threading itself between the frosted lanterns at the bridge’s apex and pulling reality tight like a drawn bow.

From the span of the **Bridge of Vows**, the portal looked like an upright frame set against the night: four feet wide, seven feet tall, and filled with a surface of pure black. Not shadow, not darkness—something flatter, absolute, as if the world ended cleanly at that boundary.

The hired flautist kept playing. Her fingers never stopped. The moment her breath faltered, the black would surely collapse, and no one on the bridge wanted to test what would happen to those already beyond.

Zeki and Kawill went first. The rest followed.

## The Thunderdome Chamber

Passing through the frame felt like stepping into deep water—pressure and cold for a heartbeat—then the world snapped back into solidity.

They stood in a domed chamber that reminded Bar’cal of a thunderdome arena: a broad circle of floor and curving walls, lit by a soft yellow glow that seemed to come from nowhere and everywhere. Along the walls, oval doors were set at intervals—sealed, silent, and too evenly spaced to feel accidental.

At the center of the room sat a curving desk of bronze, worn smooth in places like a tool handled for centuries. Three cloudy glass plates—each the size of a dinner platter—were set flush into the desk’s surface. Above them yawned a circular inset, nearly three feet across, like a viewing well or a lens that had lost its focus. A chair faced the desk: padded in spongy material, balanced strangely on a single wheel.

Drawers were built into the desk. Slots—thin, inch-wide cuts—were worked into both desk and walls with the precision of something made to accept exact shapes, not improvisation. Even the gaps looked designed.

To the left, a tapered pedestal supported a bronze coffin. Its top was transparent, and a dim blue light glowed from within, revealing the coffin’s emptiness. A panel ran along the side—lights, buttons, and more narrow slots, as if the coffin expected devices to be inserted.

Opposite the portal, a blank wall waited. It looked like a door without a handle—more suggestion than seam.

On the portal frame itself, someone found another receptacle: a hexagonal socket set into the side, currently empty.

Zeki’s eyes moved from mechanism to mechanism like a starving man in a pantry.

"**Earthwardens**," he said at last, voice tight. "Old. Older than you want it to be."

He did not elaborate, but it was enough.

## The Box and the Key

They brought the wooden box to the desk.

It was stubbornly simple at first glance—a cylinder of wood banded by five rotating rings—until the flute’s influence made its hidden work visible. Under the ongoing song from the bridge on the other side of the portal, faint patterns illuminated across the wood, and the rings’ proper alignment revealed itself like a puzzle finally admitting its rules.

With careful turns, the rings clicked into sequence.

The cap at one end released.

Inside lay a key: six inches of silver-white metal shaped like an old skeleton key, too clean, too cold, and far too precise for anything made by human hands. The inner lining of the box was metal, intended to cradle the key and keep it from shifting.

Zeki did not touch it yet. He watched everyone else instead.

## Drawers of Glass and Stone

The desk drawers yielded their own quiet haul.

On the left: a second flute made of dark flute in shape and feel, eerily similar to the one being played above. It rested in padded material alongside four smoky hexagonal crystals, each about two inches long, pointed at one end and capped at the other with metal etched in runes.

On the right: a large coin, nearly two and a half inches across, cut from greenish stone. One face bore a griffin—lion’s body, eagle’s head and wings—while the back was ringed with runes. Alongside it sat six clear crystals an inch long, and one red crystal, all capped and inscribed.

The coffin already held a purple-glowing crystal in one of its slots.

Zeki’s spellwork—quiet, precise—confirmed what his instincts shouted:

“The desk is a communicator,” he said. “Intelligence gathering. Earthwarden craft… fifty thousand years, perhaps more. The coffin—older still. A healing chamber.”

When a clear crystal was placed into a desk slot, the three-foot circular inset shimmered, and an image rose on one of the glass plates: an upper body, pale-skinned, with coal-black hair and lizard-like pupils. Six-fingered hands moved as the figure spoke, but the language was dead to them.

It did not respond.

It simply *played,* like a recording meant for an audience that no longer existed.

## Nalini Comes Through

The portal flickered. The perfect black surface sputtered and went cloudy—iced-over for a heartbeat—then three shapes stumbled into the chamber.

For an instant the room felt smaller, as if the dome had tightened its ribs around them. The yellow light did not change, yet every shadow seemed to remember it could be a hiding place. On the far side of the frame, the flute’s melody still threaded through the bridge like a held breath; here, where the air was colder and tasted faintly of metal, the sound arrived muffled—proof that the world outside was still real, still reachable, if they did not lose it.

And they were losing it. The portal’s blackness thinned in patches, stuttering between impossible dark and that cloudy, iced-over translucence. The edge of the frame gave a faint, stressed vibration, like metal under too much tension.

One was a woman in armor, masked in lace over half her face, the visible skin scarred on the same side as her mask. Two Yinka men followed, their own faces scarred to match, half-blinded by old wounds.

The woman’s head snapped up, eyes wide. "What is this place?" she demanded.

**Amaraq** recognized her at once.

"**Nalini**," he said. "*Beggar Queen.*"

Nalini’s stare cut to him, then to Zeki, then to the mechanisms. Her breathing quickened with the restrained panic of someone who had kicked down a door and found a stairwell that continued far deeper than expected.

She kept her hands close to her weapons, but her eyes never stopped moving—door, desk, coffin, slots in the wall—counting exits and counting threats, trying to decide which were the same thing.

“We took the bridge back,” she said. “The Locusts are beaten—scattered. We came to make sure whatever’s hidden here stays ours.”

Zeki did not look impressed.

His eyes flicked once—quick, calculating—to the portal’s unstable surface.

“Leave,” he snapped to the party. “Now.”

The portal collapsed.

The black thinned to a cloudy, iced translucence—then to nothing at all. For a heartbeat the bronze frame hummed with strain, like a bell struck and smothered, and then the last connection to the bridge was gone.

Nalini grabbed him by the collar.

The motion was too fast for courtesy and too controlled for fear. The lace mask brushed his beard; the metal of her gauntlet clicked against the bronze of the Earthwarden desk.

“How do we get out?”

## The Coin Door

They noticed the wall-door again. Up close, it had more honesty: a hexagonal slot at the side, and—set into the wall nearby—a circular indentation about the exact diameter of the stone coin.

Bar’cal studied the seams and nodded. “Panels,” he murmured. “A door that comes apart.”

Zeki, jaw clenched, pushed the coin into the indentation. The wall responded: panels slid forward, then aside, as if a section of the chamber had been waiting patiently to remember it could open. Beyond lay a second room.

## The Prince in the Center

The new chamber was larger—roughly fifty feet across—circular and unnervingly *perfect.* It did not feel like the rustic fitted green blocks the Y’kin carved their cities from, or even like the ancient stone of the Bridge of Vows.

This was Earthwarden work of a stranger kind: the same greenish substance, but poured in immense floor-to-ceiling slabs, seamless as if the room had been cast like concrete in a single careful breath. The stone was like the bridge and unlike it—too smooth, too precise, too indifferent to time.

The walls rose only ten feet before giving way to a shallow dome, twenty-five feet high at its apex. Overhead, the dome depicted the sky above-ground with an accuracy that made the skin prickle—moons and stars laid out as if the roof were open to the night. It was not a painting so much as an argument that the world above should be present here.

That false sky provided the light.

Seven deep, narrow seams—only a quarter-inch wide, a full inch deep—divided the wall into equal sections from floor to the base of the dome. Those seams continued into the floor, thin trenches converging toward the room’s center, as if the geometry was designed to draw every eye, every footstep, every decision toward one point.

Kindat runes covered several of the wall sections—ancient script cut with patient certainty—while two other sections had been overpainted with rough Zor writing and symbols, the brushwork crude against the Earthwarden stone. Much of the paint had flaked away, leaving faded ghosts of devotion and warning and the occasional sharper stroke where someone had pressed harder, desperate to be remembered.

Perhaps surprisingly, the air did not feel stale. It was clean in the way sealed places sometimes are—cleaned by time rather than tended by hands. Only near the bodies at the center did a faint odor linger.

“Zorian,” Zeki muttered, and then, quieter, “Late Second Era.”

At the center sat a stone sarcophagus eight feet long and four wide, made of bolted slabs clearly not native to the chamber’s construction. The bolts were rusted and fragile; the lid sat slightly askew, as if the chamber itself had shrugged off the indignity of being repurposed as a tomb.

All along the edge of the lid, Rhayan runes had been cut into the stone, readable.

Zeki read them aloud, each word making the air feel heavier:

“Here lies our lord Prince Andak of Zor, who fell defending the land of his ally. He slew many demons and servants of the Dark God Akalatan. May Eissa gently take his spirit.”

Around the sarcophagus lay the decayed remnants of five wooden coffins and the scattered bones of five men—armor rusted into red-brown lace, weapons little more than shapes.

Nalini’s men began picking through the remains with the casual greed of people who had never seen a curse wake up.

There was no time for an argument.

They took what they could from the first room—crystals, the coin, anything that felt like it belonged to the same system—and moved.

Bar’cal pocketed the purple-glowing crystal. Others gathered the clear and red crystals. Zeki kept the coin close.

## The Green Room and the Betrayal

A second door opened with the same circular coin indentation. It led to a small rectangular chamber, roughly eight feet by six by eight, carved from seamless green stone; the floor was a metal grate, and on the far side stood another green stone door.

Zeki stepped inside. The coin indentation was present again—this time on the inside of the door. He placed the coin, and the door slid shut behind him.

Outside, a different door opened—not back into the dome chamber, but into a tunnel.

Nalini’s tension snapped into motion. Steel flashed.

Zeki stunned Nalini, but the fight came anyway: she was stabbed in the leg, grappled by Bar’cal, then thrown hard by Kawill. Her two scarred men—berserkers by the look of their eyes and the way they moved—crashed into the party with brutal purpose.

It happened in the worst kind of light: not dark enough to hide, not bright enough to see cleanly. The yellow glow caught on blades and teeth and the wet of breath. The chamber amplified every sound—boots scraping on stone, the dull ring of metal on bone, the sharp little gasp when someone realized a strike had landed deeper than intended.

Lightning blew one of them apart. The other died under knives and blades.

Nalini did not survive the end.

When it was over, breaths came ragged. The room was suddenly too quiet.

Amaraq went down hard.

For a moment no one spoke his name. There was only the slick sound of blood where it should not have been, and the awful, widening certainty that if they did not solve the Earthwarden relics *now,* he would die here—on an ancient floor that did not care whether men lived or ended.

Zeki emerged, and in the aftermath—whether out of necessity or habit—he gathered what Nalini carried: a rapier, a veil, a belt heavy with money, and three small vials filled with the same light chartreuse liquid.

No one argued.

## The Healing Coffin

Amaraq had taken the worst of it—worse than anyone wanted to admit out loud.

His skin had gone the wrong color. His breathing came shallow and uneven, like a bellows with a cracked seam; each inhale sounded borrowed.

They returned to the bronze coffin and laid him within, the blue light bathing his torn flesh in something that felt like moonlit water.

The coffin was too clean for the blood they were smearing across it. Too calm. It accepted his weight without complaint, as if it had been built for this exact shape of desperation.

Bar’cal returned the purple crystal to its slot.

The coffin’s glow deepened. Nothing happened at first.

Seconds stretched thin. The blue light trembled along the lid like a reflection on deep water. Zeki watched the panel and its tiny marks with the rigid focus of a man trying not to flinch.

When Amaraq drew breath again, it sounded like a man waking from drowning.

No one called it a miracle.

But when his eyes finally moved—when the panic of the room loosened by a single notch—they traded looks that said the same thing: they had no right to have gotten that lucky.

## Andak Wakes

In the dome chamber, someone finally lifted the sarcophagus lid. Cold rolled out of it, and something began to materialize—undead presence forming like condensation in the air. The five sets of bones around the sarcophagus shuddered.

Rust-stiff ribs lifted. Fingers re-knit themselves from scattered phalanges. Skulls turned as if listening for a command that had been spoken centuries ago and had only now arrived.

The fight came fast and ugly—steel ringing against old bone, sparks off stone, breath clouding in the cold that spilled from the tomb.

They broke skeletons the way you break dry wood: a hard blow, a crack, a collapse. But there were five of them, and the room was wide, and the party was already frayed to the edge from Nalini’s betrayal and Amaraq’s near death. Every heartbeat meant another step backward.

And behind them—at the edge of lantern light and certainty—the thing in the sarcophagus *stood without standing.* A barrow-wight’s presence, half in the air, half in memory, pressing against the room like a storm front.

They tried to meet it with steel.

Blades passed through cold like striking fog. The resistance was wrong—too light—and the recoil that followed was worse: a numbness that crawled up the wrists, as if something on the other side of the cut had briefly remembered their names.

Against *it,* normal weapons were not weapons at all.

The party fell back into the small green chamber, slamming the coin-door shut between them and the terrible patience of the dead.

For a moment, it seemed the door would not hold. Something cold and furious tested the seam; the grate beneath their feet thrummed like a plucked wire.

Zeki’s spellwork flared—more a desperate ward than a killing blow—and the pressure eased just enough for the mechanism to remember it could move.

The far door slid open.

They ran.

Behind them, the clatter of bone echoed—distant, then nearer, then swallowed by the tunnel’s turns. And over it all, fading as the distance grew, a voice scraped through the air from the dome chamber:

“The Iron Wind… is coming again…”

They fled into the tunnel’s promise, carrying a handful of salvaged wonders and a growing certainty that the Bridge of Vows was less a crossing than a machine—one that answered to bargains, to keys, and to whoever had the will to feed it.

Behind them, the domed ceiling’s painted stars watched without blinking.
