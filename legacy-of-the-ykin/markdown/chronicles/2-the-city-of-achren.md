# Chapter 2: The City of **Achren**

They found the smoke before they found the voices. The road dipped, and ahead the air held that sharp, familiar mix of burned wood and cooking fat—campfire life, but also campfire trouble. By late afternoon the light had gone buttery and low, and it made the haze above the trees look almost peaceful.

It wasn’t.

A Yinka stepped out to bar their way, claiming he walked apart from his own people. He demanded a “small contribution,” as if shaking down travelers was a kind of honest work. Tension tightened in the group the way a bowstring tightens: quiet at first, then suddenly too much.

Bloth’roc slid his battleax free, and Nawiss spoke to the living world—plants snapping up to snare.

The bandit tried to posture through it. He didn’t get the chance. Caught off guard, he stumbled—an ugly, ordinary trip that ended his story. Bloth’roc’s axe did the rest.

Then the real commotion crested the hill. Armand saw Kulkan first—wearing Commander Aapo’s face—and for a heartbeat it could have meant reinforcements or a second ambush. Kulkan wasn’t alone. Three unfamiliar figures moved with him: an older Borley graduate with the hard look of someone who’d survived examinations more dangerous than paper, another armed with a distinctive dual crossbow, and a silent ragged figure whose stillness read as a threat.

Their barrage turned the skirmish into a rout. Whatever hostilities remained in the camp died quickly and without ceremony.

They camped that night with the kind of vigilance that followed easy violence. By dawn, the road had become a current—traffic thickening, voices carrying ahead on the wind—and the gates of **Achren** rose out of smoke and stone.

The group had grown large: **Kulkan**, **Beltharr**, **Kawill**, **Bloth’roc**, and **Armand**, plus **Kulkan**’s new companions—**Bar’cal** (an assassin from **Borley**), **Babajide** (an assassin and sniper from the **Tikal Cloisters** of **Ixtal**), and **Eadrich**, an administrator or scholar.

The last miles before the gate felt less like an approach and more like being absorbed. The road was lined with makeshift stalls and traveling merchants, an improvised market clinging to the city’s shadow: bolts of bright cloth hung from poles; spice-sellers fanned their wares into small, fragrant dunes; cages and pens shifted with livestock that didn’t like the crush. The air came layered—fresh produce, baked sweetness, sweat, and that blunt animal smell underneath everything.

At the gate, the city proved it could still be a machine. Guards checked carts and travelers with practiced suspicion, hands quick on ledgers and straps. Scribes sat at a narrow table just inside the archway, recording names and goods as if ink could keep order where the **Priest-Lords** had once done it with fear.

Then the wall swallowed them and the sound doubled.

Inside, the space opened into a bustling square that beat like a heart—vendors haggling, coin clinking, laughter and argument braided together until it was hard to tell where one ended and the other began. Children ran between legs in impromptu games, darting around sacks and wheels and sharp-tempered adults. Elderly citizens clustered on a stone bench, trading anecdotes and cackling as if the city had decided—today, at least—that it would not mourn.

Two shops stood out by their own insistence. An apothecary called **Scented Whispers** wore hanging herbs like trophies, its door never still as citizens slipped in and out for remedies and quiet poisons. Farther down, **Bread of Bliss** vented warmth and yeast into the street; its window displayed a golden bounty that made hunger feel like an insult.

And yet, threaded through the cheer, there was the absence: fewer young men than there should have been in a square this lively. The joy was real—but it carried a shadow that moved when the crowd moved.

**Achren** sat where the **Cohoa** cut the land open—part city, part wound. In spring, meltwater made the river below the gorge roar and churn, a violent ribbon that seemed to insist the world was still moving even when men tried to freeze it into law. Later in the year the waters would retreat, exposing lush green banks and sudden life, but now the sound of it carried up to the walls in a constant, impatient rush.

Even from the road, the **Bridge of Vows** was impossible to ignore. It arched over the **Cohoa** in green-gray stone fitted so precisely there was no mortar to be seen, and at either end cliff-cat statues stood sentinel—half warning, half blessing—staring down anyone who meant to cross. Travelers spoke of the bridge the way they spoke of weather: you could pretend it wasn’t there, but it still shaped everything you did in **Achren**.

## Respite at Tameki House

They didn’t linger. Almost as soon as they cleared the gate and caught the square’s noise and color, a young boy—quick-eyed, practiced—slid to their side and guided them out before anyone could decide they looked worth noticing. The city’s cheer clung to the streets like perfume, but the boy kept them moving.

Achren had shifted since Winter’s end, 6049, when the Priest-Lords departed—an absence that left a vacuum gangs, militias, and would-be civic leaders were still arguing over. The rules hadn’t vanished; they’d become negotiable, depending on who was watching.

He led them to **Tameki House**, a roadside inn that promised warmth and a roof without questions.

The hearth did what hearths always did: it made strangers soften around the edges. Tamekis padded through the common room, feline companions with calm eyes and a way of making violence feel—briefly—like a rumor.

Inside waited a familiar knot of lives: the enigmatic **Kawill**; **Akto** and **Bembe Tun**, steady as fenceposts; and **Zuma**, ragged in a way that suggested either hard travel or hard secrets.

Upon entering, Beltharr’s stoic facade momentarily shattered and he collapsed into a wave of raw emotion. Crumbling beneath the weight of some inner turmoil, tears flowed and sobs wracked his body. The Tun brothers, as if understanding something others did not, offered consolation and shared some quiet words until Beltharr regained his composure.

Xuloc, the innkeeper, moved through the room with quiet grace and an ease that suggested she’d seen every kind of trouble and learned which ones to greet with a smile. She let them settle in as if Tameki House had been built for this exact kind of temporary alliance.

Night brought Pachamanca—meat and vegetables cooked down into deep comfort. Heat, spice, and fat soothed the nerves that the road had rubbed raw.

One of the Tamekis took to Bar’cal. It lingered near his boots, calm and attentive, and Xuloc—matter-of-fact—offered it to him as a companion. (They were for sale, she added, and worth it.)

Bar’cal refused, sharply enough to show a seam in his composure.

Even with food and fire, Kulkan’s impatience didn’t leave. It sat in him like a pebble in a boot.

As the evening deepened, Denivor finally laid the heart of it on the table. He intended to leave with Bembe and Zuma—to hunt the Implementor, a force of imminent destruction. But Denivor wanted Nawiss in particular. The Tun brothers had spoken of her, and Denivor believed she had a pivotal role to play.

He also named another pressure in the world: the Cult of Ezran—fervent adherents of the Implementor’s cause.

Bloth’roc, pulled by whatever destiny he listened to, declared he would go with Nawiss.

Kulkan agreed, but his consent carried the scrape of reluctance.

## Shifting Allegiances

Morning came clean and cold. Denivor, Zuma, Nawiss, Bloth’roc, and Bembe Tun left Tameki House to begin their hunt. Akto Tun saw them off with a promise to reunite later—simple words offered like a handhold over a drop.

Back in **Achren**, **Kulkan** moved fast. He sent word to **Amaraq**, the contact who held information about **Zeki Balaban**. The rendezvous point was the **Achren Temple** grounds.

The temple grounds should have held incense and low voices. Instead there was only absence.

The priests were gone, and what remained felt like a place that had been hollowed out and left standing for appearances. Amaraq waited there with a contingent of local militia, guarding the perimeter the way men guard a story they don’t fully believe.

Kulkan—still wearing Commander Aapo—let his impatience show. He demanded the key from Armand.

Armand refused.

The standoff broke into violence with the speed of something that had been waiting all along. Kawill rebelled. Beltharr hesitated, then turned on Kulkan. Bar’cal joined them. Kulkan found himself outnumbered and suddenly vulnerable.

In Achren, even an argument could become a contract if it happened on the wrong stones. Everyone knew that now—or thought they did. Once the bridge’s power had been exposed to the city at large, the old currency of favors and threats had been joined by a new one: phrasing. People spoke carefully around anyone who looked like a broker or fixer, the sort who could steer a conversation toward the bridge and make words stick. It made the empty temple grounds feel stranger; silence, too, had become a weapon.

Beltharr nearly ended Kulkan there.

Eadrich’s spell pulled Kulkan away at the last moment—snatching him out of the consequence he’d earned.

Then the world tore.

A monstrous demon emerged through a rift and turned the temple grounds into a slaughterhouse. It moved with ferocious purpose. Two soldiers died. Babajide died.

The rest ran.

## A New Path

**Solitude Tavern** offered cover, not comfort. There, with the air still tasting faintly of panic, **Amaraq** explained **Achren**’s new realities: the city’s strange shift in power since the **Priest-Lords** had gone, and the way the **Bridge of Vows** sat behind it all like an engine humming under the stones.

While they talked, Dirtka arrived—carrying answers for Armand’s long-held questions, and an invitation that was also a knife: a journey back toward Ixtal, and toward Armand’s estranged mother. Armand accepted, and left with Dirtka.

Amaraq’s explanation had an edge to it: Achren had not become honest—only *literal*. Deals made on the Bridge of Vows could bind, enforce, and ruin. That had created a new class of small-time authorities: vow-crafters and mediators who sold clean language the way others sold steel. Some were respectable. Some were fixers. All of them understood that one bad sentence could start a feud.

Zeki Balaban, a recluse on the east side of the Bridge of Vows, was the next necessity.

To reach him, they needed the **Afflicted**—beggar-network and information brokers, rulers of the east in ways polite maps didn’t show. **Amaraq** arranged the passage and brought in **Xochipilli**, a beggar and a member of the **Afflicted**.

The Afflicted were not merely beggars, despite the name the upworld used. They were a network—catacombs and tunnels beneath Achren, a system of watchers and whisperers who traded in information more often than coin. And they had their own grievance stitched into the city’s new era: the gem that empowered the bridge had been stolen from them in the upheaval that drove out the Priest-Lords. Whether the Locusts had taken it directly or merely arranged the theft, the result was the same. The wound between Afflicted and Locusts never fully closed; some nights they aligned, other nights they plotted each other’s ruin.

They crossed into the Afflicted’s territory and felt the city change.

The streets narrowed. Eyes watched from places that didn’t look like windows. The air smelled older—stone, damp, and secrets.

They found Kulkan’s body.

The Captain stood nearby—burly, missing an arm, built like someone who’d once been meant for sieges and had decided to apply the same thinking to alleys and tunnels. He declared Kulkan’s corpse and belongings a gift.

With The Captain and his guide, Taloc, they threaded the winding streets toward Zeki’s secluded workshop.

Zeki Balaban was old and reclusive, and his workshop felt like a place where sunlight went to die.

He had remained secluded for years, oblivious to the city’s transformations—or pretending to be. The mysterious box and its elusive key drew him out instantly. He wanted its secrets.

But the box could not be opened without a flute—long since stolen, but still somewhere in Achren.

Zeki insisted on accompanying them. He gathered his belongings with surprising speed, as if he’d been waiting for an excuse to leave the room without admitting it.

When they turned back toward the bridge, the Afflicted emerged from the shadows in numbers that made the air feel crowded.

They wanted something.

## The Captain’s Gambit

From Zeki they learned the bridge could be used like a device.

A special flute, paired with a specific piece of sheet music, could create a powerful localized Essænce focus on the Bridge of Vows. That performance—done right—would allow the box to open.

As they left Zeki’s home, The Captain appeared again—one-armed and commanding, with the calm certainty of a man used to being obeyed.

“I’m very glad you had the chance to speak with Zeki,” he said. “We can help you on your journey, but first, we need something from you before you leave here.”

The gem that powered the bridge was a cultural artifact of immense importance to the Afflicted—stolen from them by the Locusts.

The Captain demanded they retrieve it.

The flute, he said, would also allow access to a secret room—one that might hold items of great significance. His request left no room for refusal, and the party felt the weight of the Afflicted’s presence surrounding them.

It was the first time the bridge stopped being just a landmark in their minds and became what it actually was in Achren: a lever. A tool powerful enough to rearrange the city, and dangerous enough that every faction wanted it—or wanted it broken. If the Afflicted believed the bridge’s power had been taken from them, then any “help” they offered came with a hook hidden in the kindness.

Back at Solitude Tavern, they planned over sunfire brew and harvest pie.

During the meal, Beltharr spoke a truth that changed the shape of his name.

“You might have known me as Beltharr,” he said, “but I killed my identical brother, Belthor. Looking into a mirror, I realized I had assumed his persona. I am Belthor. He was the more agreeable and social one, and this is who I choose to be now.”

It landed like a dropped cup in a quiet room. The admission sharpened the tension in the group as they debated their options.

Zeki reminded them that using the flute on the bridge would be a highly visible act—something factions would notice.

They chose theater.

They hired Tlanextli and Citlalin—musicians from Solitude—to play the flute and the sheet music on the bridge.

Before they could perform, trouble came to them.

Laurnall Joyet—Amaraq’s archnemesis—arrived with his irregulars, smug and hungry for leverage. He wanted Kulkan’s possessions. He taunted them about their recent activities, as if he’d been watching the whole time.

Zeki ended the talking by casting a sleep spell on Nermal, one of Laurnall’s men.

The tavern erupted.

But the irregulars weren’t ready for a group already hardened by betrayal and a demon at the temple. Bar’cal pinned Nermal in a headlock. Kawill delivered decisive blows. The rest followed in the same ruthless rhythm.

When the violence settled, Zeki interrogated Nermal and dragged truth out of him: the Locusts had a double agent within the Afflicted named Taloqin, and Ichar—his employer—sought the skull the group carried.

The next day, they met The Captain at Zaal Plaza to finalize their plans.

He offered a counterproposal: open the secret room, retrieve the items, but leave the flute and the sheet music behind when they departed. He reiterated the importance of keeping them out of enemy hands.

He also apologized—insincerely—for Amaraq’s past ordeals.

## The Ritual on the Bridge

Their preparations complete, they went to Kolat’s Forbidden Emporium to acquire the flute.

Zeki clashed with Kolat, a former partner, and the air between them held the old bitterness of shared work gone wrong. But coin changed hands, words were spoken carefully, and the transaction was ultimately successful.

That evening, under cover of night, they approached the Bridge of Vows.

Lanterns cast a soft glow through frosted glass, turning the bridge’s green-gray stone into something colder, older. Tlanextli and Citlalin raised the flute and began to play the sheet music.

The melody resonated across the span.

As it swelled, swirling colors emerged between two lanterns at the bridge’s apex. The colors tightened into an oval portal, four feet wide and seven feet tall. The swirling hues collapsed into an ominous pitch-black void.

Most citizens thought of the Bridge of Vows as a social engine—truth, bargains, enforced oaths. What the group was attempting was something rarer and far more volatile: using the bridge as an Essænce focus, a deliberate ritual that treated its old systems like machinery. It wasn’t common knowledge in Achren; it was the kind of secret that could get a person killed, or recruited, depending on which faction heard it first.

Zeki and Kawill stepped through first.

Amaraq followed. Then Bar’cal. Then Beltharr.

Inside, they found themselves in a vast chamber shaped like a thunderdome, softly illuminated with yellow light. Oval doors lined the curved walls. At the center sat a metal desk with hexagonal indentations, drawers built into its body as if it had been grown rather than constructed, and nearby a pedestal held a bronze coffin.

Crystals waited in a quiet, deliberate arrangement: various shapes, various colors—tools for a system that no longer had caretakers.

When they placed a white crystal into the desk’s indentations, a projection stirred to life: a six-fingered, lizard-eyed figure with coal-black hair speaking in an unknown language.

The coffin appeared empty.

Its stillness did not feel like safety.
